Part 1: how to answer the podcast questions

You get one question per VIP. Use P.E.E.L. every time:

  • Point: name the VIP and their idea in one sentence.
  • Evidence: a specific moment from the transcript (who said what, or a short quote).
  • Explain: say exactly how that moment shows the idea.
  • Link: the effect (stereotype, habit, different reading, who benefits) or the missing perspective.

What to listen for

VIPHunt for these in the transcript
Said"Us vs. them"; a group called backward, dangerous, exotic or helpless; one group speaking for another; the West as the default or hero; who is never heard.

Fill-in starters

  • Said: "Said's idea of the Other shows up when the speaker describes ___ as ___. This frames ___ as ___ and the West as ___, which can lead to ___. The voice missing is ___."
  • McLuhan: "McLuhan says the medium is the message. Because this is delivered as ___, it encourages ___ (habit), so audiences ___ regardless of the actual content."
  • Hall: "The producer encodes ___ through the word '___'. A listener who ___ might give a dominant reading, while someone who ___ might give a negotiated or oppositional one."
  • Chomsky: "The filter of ___ appears when ___. This shapes the story by ___ and sidelines ___."
Always give 2 pieces of evidence if you can, and always say why it matters. "It shows bias" alone earns little.

Perspective basics (slides 1 to 24)

Where perspectives come from

  • Socio-economic status (SES): your position in the community, set by parents' jobs, income, education, neighbourhood.
  • Life experiences: gender, ethnicity, age, religion and more.
  • Perspectives change as people live through new things or get older. A person can be "left wing" on one issue and very different on another, so the political spectrum is not a single line for each person.

Why point of view matters

  • Better understanding of others' opinions, beliefs, values.
  • Conflict resolution: seeking different views helps settle arguments.
  • Including others: notice which views are present and which are missing.

Stakeholders: Downtown Guelph encampments

Stakeholders on the slide: business owners, downtown citizens, those experiencing homelessness, city council/mayor. For each you gave a rationale, rated how useful their view is (1 to 5), and noted where to learn more about their perspective.

Hard empathy, strawman, steelman

  • Easy empathy: understanding people who think like you. Hard empathy: understanding people who don't, without agreeing or excusing. You don't have to agree to understand.
  • Strawman: making the other side's argument weaker so it's easy to attack ("They're just stupid", "They don't care about people", "They want to ruin the country"). It happens when we skip hard empathy.
  • Steelman: the strongest, fairest version of the argument you disagree with ("I understand why someone might think that because...", "A fair concern they have is...", "The strongest version of their point is...").
  • Strawman: shuts down discussion, creates division, you learn nothing, you stay in your bubble. Steelman: builds hard empathy, shows maturity, shows the real issue, makes your own arguments better.
  • Key line: steelmanning is empathy for someone's reasoning, not necessarily their conclusion. A steelman also needs the best evidence.
  • Practice topics: school uniforms; Canada increasing military spending (Side A vs Side B).

Facts vs. opinions

  • Opinions: beliefs about events; form when facts are viewed through your values; subjective (shaped by perspective).
  • Facts: certain, concrete, verifiable by observation and measurement; your experience can't change them; objective.

Critical consumers of news

  • Activity: Academy of Paediatrics vs. American College of Paediatricians, to decide which source is more credible.
  • Vertical reading: judging a source by staying on its own page and website (the first-round activity).
  • Lateral reading: leaving the page and opening other sites to see who the source is and what others say about it (Crash Course video). Ask what new info you found, which sites helped most, and whether your view changed.

Vertical/lateral definitions are the standard ones; your slides only show the activities.

The single story (Adichie)

  • Single story: the same story told over and over about a people or place we don't know first-hand. Danger: stereotypes and half-truths, not the full truth.
  • Power = telling a story and making it the definitive story. Links to race, gender, class. Aim for a balance of stories.
  • Africa activity: "What comes to mind... Africa" showed safaris and wildlife, tribal imagery, child soldier, starving child, a magazine cover, the "starving in Africa" meme. Then the slides flipped to skylines, a football match, shopping, middle-class families, big cities: the missing stories.
  • Examples of the single story: a 1561 London merchant (John Locke in Adichie's talk) calling Africans beasts with no houses; Kipling's writing casting the British Empire as a force for civilization.

VIP 1: Edward Said (1935 to 2003)

  • Palestinian-American intellectual, literary theorist, cultural critic. Key book: Orientalism.
  • Argument: Western scholarship and literature often depict the East (Middle East, Asia, "the Orient") as exotic, inferior, and as "the Other". This supports colonial and imperialist ideas.
  • Question the sources of knowledge and how power and ideology shape the narratives that form our view of the world.
  • Examples on the slides: Locke's account; Kipling; modern pop culture (Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom, Homeland, American Sniper, Aladdin, Call of Duty: Modern Warfare); a "Western perspective" video clip; world maps (Mercator vs. Equal Earth, the UN vote to adopt a map showing Africa's true size, flipped and re-centred maps), showing that even "north on top" is a perspective choice.
  • Guiding question: How do media representations shape how we see people from other parts of the world?
  • Takeaway: stereotypical, biased portrayals of "the Other" can feed prejudice and real-world harm, so Western-centric views should be challenged for a more nuanced understanding.

VIP 2: Marshall McLuhan

  • "The medium is the message." The medium (the means that carries information) affects how we perceive a message. It is how it's delivered, not just the content.
  • Media/technology are forces that shape behaviour and society. Shifts (print to TV to internet) have cumulative effects.
  • Quote: "We shape our tools and thereafter our tools shape us."
  • Speed of adoption (time to 100 million users): telephone 75 years (1878); mobile phone 16 (1979); World Wide Web 7 (1990); Twitter 5 (2012); Facebook 4.5 (2004); WhatsApp 3 years 4 months (2009); TikTok 9 months (2018); ChatGPT 2 months (2023).
  • Questions to ask: What habits or behaviours does this medium encourage? How does it change how we access information? Does it shape power dynamics (who controls the narrative)?
  • Same message, different medium: handwritten love letter vs. text message; mixtape/CD vs. Spotify playlist.
  • Art as a medium: Chris Hondros' war photography. Is the emotional impact from the story or from the fact it is a photograph? It would feel different as an article or documentary.
  • Internet and confirmation bias: Making Sense podcast clips (confirmation bias; Yuval Noah Harari: an abundance of information means truth often loses); Facebook's "angry" reaction; The Social Dilemma (Center for Humane Technology).
  • Social media features = McLuhan in action:
    • Algorithm-driven feeds: personalize content based on behaviour, so the medium shapes your experience.
    • Notifications and "like" button: dopamine hits that condition you to seek quick validation, building addictive habits.
    • Stories and short-form (Instagram Stories, TikTok): brief, consumable content leads to surface-level engagement and shorter attention spans.

VIP 3: Stuart Hall

  • Encoding: producers of news, TV, ads embed meanings and values reflecting certain ideologies or viewpoints.
  • Decoding: audiences interpret messages, not passively, but through their own experiences, social position, and cultural background. Cultural identity is a "lens".
  • Media doesn't just reflect the world, it helps shape our ideas.
ReadingWhat the audience does
Dominant (preferred)Takes the message exactly as the producer intended.
NegotiatedAccepts part, resists or modifies part.
OppositionalRejects it and reads it in a completely different way.

Decode the headlines (slide examples)

  • Encoding / framing: "Canada recognizes Palestinian statehood, in an abrupt reversal of foreign policy" vs. "Canada joins other countries in recognizing a Palestinian state ahead of UN General Assembly". Same event; "abrupt reversal" and "joins other countries" encode different impressions. Also a NowThis clip about on-screen banners and question wording in TV news (check your notes for the specifics).
  • Language: a CTV Montreal tweet, "At least 70 killed in Israel, 198 dead in Gaza", where one number has no cause attached (passive voice); an Intercept analysis counting words like "slaughter", "massacre", "horrific" used far more for Israeli than Palestinian deaths in the LA Times, NYT, WaPo.
  • Under-reporting / gatekeeping: an Intercept chart where mentions of Palestinians fell as the death toll rose (Oct 7 to Nov 25, 2023); an LA Times headline and article on airstrikes. Gatekeepers decide what gets covered and how much.
  • Also: Vox's "Protests aren't what they look like on TV".

These are case studies for the concepts. The test is about the idea (encoding, language, gatekeeping), not taking a side.

Takeaway: media shape perceptions and beliefs; messages are not "one size fits all" (audiences differ); media can reflect and perpetuate stereotypes and bias; media is a powerful tool that can shape society.

VIP 4: Noam Chomsky

  • MIT professor, historian, social critic, activist. Co-authored Manufacturing Consent (with Edward Herman).
  • In a democracy control can't be imposed by force, so limits are set by subtle means using media.
  • Guiding question: Who decides what stories make the news, and which never get told?
  • Core idea: media don't inform citizens impartially; they manufacture consent for the interests of powerful elites (corporations, government, influential groups). This is the propaganda model: media look independent and objective but often advance the agendas of the powerful, especially during military conflict.

Our own bias (slides 109 to 112)

  • Bias: a tendency to believe some people or ideas are better than others, often leading to unfair treatment.
  • Explicit bias: attitudes we consciously hold and express, positive or negative. Can contradict implicit bias.
  • Implicit bias: attitudes outside our awareness and control; everyone has them, even judges and journalists, built over a lifetime; they can be combatted and changed.
  • Confirmation bias: we favour information that confirms existing beliefs and discount what disagrees.
  • Availability bias: thinking examples that come easily to mind are more representative than they really are.
  • Negativity bias: negative things affect us more than equally intense neutral or positive things.
  • Documentary study: Control Room (about how different media cover the same events).

Self-test (tap to reveal)

1. "The medium is the message" means...How content is delivered shapes us more than the content itself (McLuhan).
2. Which VIP gives dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings?Stuart Hall.
3. A fast-food ad sponsor discourages critical stories about fast food. Which filter?Filter 2, advertising revenue (Chomsky).
4. Authors who portray the East as exotic and inferior are doing what, per Said?Orientalism: presenting the East as "the Other", supporting colonial ideas.
5. Strawman vs. steelman?Strawman weakens the other side's argument to attack it; steelman states it in its strongest, fairest form, even if you disagree.
6. Difference between a fact and an opinion?Facts are objective and verifiable; opinions are subjective and shaped by perspective and values.
7. Which bias makes dramatic, easily recalled examples seem typical?Availability bias.
8. Name the 5 filters.Ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak, common enemy.
9. Why are algorithm feeds and "likes" a McLuhan example?The medium's features (personalization, dopamine rewards, short-form) shape behaviour and attention regardless of content.
10. What is the danger of a single story?Stereotypes and half-truths; whoever has power makes their story the definitive one (Adichie).
11. Explicit vs. implicit bias?Explicit is conscious and deliberately held; implicit is outside awareness, and everyone has some.
12. Vertical vs. lateral reading?Vertical: judge a source from its own page. Lateral: leave the page and check what other sources say about it.
Common traps: McLuhan is about the medium, not the content. Hall's "dominant" reading means the audience agrees with the producer. Steelman does not mean you agree. Sourcing (who journalists quote) is not the same as ownership (who owns the outlet). Flak is backlash after publishing.
